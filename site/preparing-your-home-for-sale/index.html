Preparing your home for sale

The goal isn't a renovation — it's removing every reason a buyer would lower their offer. Here's what's worth doing in the St. Louis area, and what isn't.

Refresh, don't renovate

Most sellers either do too little or spend too much on the wrong things. A deep clean, neutral paint and small repairs usually return more than any remodel — buyers pay for a home that feels cared for, not for your taste in countertops. Before you spend a dollar, walk through with me: I'll tell you honestly which projects will show up in your price and which won't.

The prep checklist, in order

  1. Start the occupancy permit early

    Most towns on both sides of the river inspect the home before a new owner moves in. Knocking out those items first means fixing what the town will force you to fix anyway — on your schedule, not during the contract.

  2. Fix the inspection red flags

    Anything a buyer's inspector will flag in the first ten minutes: active leaks, bad gutters, dead outlets, a furnace that hasn't been serviced. These kill deals; countertops don't.

  3. Deep clean and kill the odors

    Buyers decide in the first minute, and smell is half of it. Carpets, pet areas, smoke — handle them before the first showing, because there's no second first impression.

  4. Paint in neutrals

    The cheapest transformation per dollar. Fresh, light, neutral walls make rooms feel bigger, brighter and newer.

  5. Refresh the curb appeal

    Most buyers see your house first as a photo on a phone. Mow, mulch, trim, and make the front door area inviting — it sets the expectation for everything inside.

  6. Small updates, not remodels

    New cabinet hardware, modern light fixtures, updated faucets. Skip the kitchen remodel — you rarely get that money back, and buyers would rather pick their own.

  7. Declutter and depersonalize

    Buyers need to picture their life in the house, not yours. Clear counters, thin out closets, pack the photo wall early — you're moving anyway.

What buyers actually notice

After hundreds of showings with first-time buyers, the pattern is consistent: the listing photos get them in the door, kitchens and baths get the longest look, neutral and clean beats updated and cluttered, odors end showings early, and a clean inspection keeps the deal together. Prepare for those five things and you've done the work that matters.

Sell as-is, or fix first?

Sometimes the right answer is to fix nothing and price accordingly — an estate sale, a house with major systems at end of life, or a seller who needs speed over dollars. That's a math question, not a pride question: we compare what the repairs cost against what they return in your price, and you decide with real numbers. This is part of the free written analysis I prepare for every seller.

How long does prep take?

A well-kept home: a weekend or two. A home with deferred maintenance: plan 30 to 90 days, mostly waiting on contractors — another reason to walk through with me early, even months before you plan to list.

Prep questions sellers ask

Should I remodel my kitchen before selling?

Almost never. Full remodels rarely return their cost at sale, and buyers often prefer choosing their own finishes. Clean, declutter, update hardware and lighting, and let the price reflect the kitchen's age instead.

What should I fix first before listing?

Start with what your town's occupancy inspection will require, then anything a buyer's inspector will flag: leaks, electrical issues, HVAC service, roof and gutter problems. Cosmetics come after function.

Is staging worth it?

Full professional staging makes sense for some vacant homes. For most occupied homes, decluttering, depersonalizing, good light and furniture arranged to show the space get you most of the benefit for free. I'll tell you which camp your home is in when I see it.

How much does it cost to get a home ready to sell?

For a well-kept home, often just elbow grease and a few hundred dollars of paint and hardware. With deferred maintenance it varies too much to guess — which is exactly why the walk-through comes before the spending.

Can you recommend contractors and handymen?

Yes. Through years of transactions and my referral network I keep a current list of painters, handymen, inspectors and contractors my clients have used — ask me.
