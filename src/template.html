Step by step

How buying your first home works

Seven steps from first conversation to keys. Most buyers take 30 to 90 days from pre-approval to closing. Want to see it in real life? Read Paige’s story.

  1. Talk through your goals

    We cover your budget, the monthly payment you're comfortable with, your timeline and your must-haves. No cost and no pressure.

  2. Get pre-approved

    A lender checks your income, debts and credit and tells you how much you can borrow and which programs you qualify for. Do this before you tour, so you know your real price range and sellers take your offer seriously.

  3. Sign a buyer agreement

    Required before we tour homes together. It spells out what I do for you and how I'm paid, so there are no surprises later.

  4. Tour homes

    I set up a search that matches what you want and we tour the homes worth seeing. I'll point out the things that cost money later: roof, furnace, water heater, foundation, windows.

  5. Make an offer and negotiate

    I pull comparable sales so your offer is based on data, then negotiate price, closing-cost help and terms on your behalf.

  6. Inspection, appraisal and occupancy permit

    You hire a home inspector and we decide together what to ask the seller to repair or credit. Your lender orders the appraisal. In most towns on both sides of the river, the city also has to inspect the home and issue an occupancy permit before you move in.

  7. Close and get your keys

    We do a final walk-through, you sign at closing and the house is yours.

Myths, fears and mistakes

What holds first-time buyers back

"I need 20% down."

No. FHA loans allow 3.5% down, some conventional loans allow 3%, and USDA loans in eligible areas allow zero down. Putting less than 20% down usually means paying mortgage insurance, which is often worth it to start building equity sooner.

"My credit isn't good enough."

Many loan programs accept scores in the 600s, and the state assistance programs in Missouri and Illinois start at 640. If you're not there yet, a lender can tell you exactly what to fix and how long it should take.

"Renting is cheaper."

Sometimes, month to month. But in much of St. Louis and the Metro East, a mortgage payment on a starter home is close to rent, and every payment builds equity instead of paying someone else's mortgage.

"I should wait for rates to drop."

Nobody can time rates. Buy when the payment fits your budget today. If rates fall later, you may be able to refinance.

Mistake: shopping before pre-approval

Falling for a house you can't afford, or losing one because you weren't approved yet, is the most common first-time buyer heartbreak.

Mistake: new debt before closing

Don't finance a car, open a credit card or change jobs between pre-approval and closing. Your lender checks again right before closing, and new debt can sink your loan.

Mistake: skipping the inspection

A home inspection costs a few hundred dollars and can save you thousands. It's your chance to learn the condition of the roof, furnace, plumbing and foundation before you commit.

Mistake: forgetting the costs beyond the down payment

Closing costs, the inspection, the appraisal, moving and the first few repairs all add up. Plan for them now, or negotiate for the seller to help.

What it costs

The money you'll need, and when

Exact numbers depend on the price, the loan and the program. Your lender gives you a written Loan Estimate with the real figures.

  • Earnest money, at offer. A good-faith deposit held until closing and credited toward your purchase. Often around 1% of the price, sometimes less.
  • Home inspection, right after the offer is accepted. Usually a few hundred dollars, paid to the inspector. Radon, sewer scope and termite checks cost extra.
  • Appraisal, during the loan process. Paid through your lender. It confirms the home is worth what you're paying.
  • Down payment, at closing. 0% to 3.5% for many first-time buyers, depending on the loan.
  • Closing costs, at closing. Lender fees, title insurance, recording fees and prepaid taxes and insurance. Commonly 2% to 5% of the price. Sellers can agree to pay part of them, and assistance programs can cover more.

First-time buyer FAQ

Questions buyers ask me most

If yours isn't here, call or text 314-494-6038.

How much house can I afford?

Start with the monthly payment you're comfortable with, including taxes and insurance, not the maximum a lender approves. In Illinois, property taxes add more to the payment than in Missouri, so compare payments, not prices. A lender's pre-approval gives you the real ceiling.

Do I need 20% down?

No. FHA loans allow 3.5% down, some conventional loans 3%, and USDA loans in eligible areas zero. Missouri's MHDC and Illinois's IHDA programs can help with the down payment and closing costs.

What credit score do I need?

It depends on the loan, but many first-time buyer programs start at 640, and FHA can go lower. A lender can review your credit and tell you exactly where you stand.

What counts as a first-time buyer?

For most programs, it means you haven't owned a home in the last three years, so past owners can qualify too. Each program sets its own rules.

What does it cost to work with a buyer's agent?

Commissions are negotiable. Before we tour homes, we sign a buyer agreement that spells out exactly how I'm paid. In many deals the seller covers some or all of it, and I'll explain your options up front.

How much are closing costs?

Commonly 2% to 5% of the price. Your lender gives you a written Loan Estimate. Sellers can agree to pay part of them, and down-payment programs can help with the rest.

How long does it take to buy a home?

Searching can take a few weeks or a few months. Once your offer is accepted, closing usually takes 30 to 45 days.

Should I buy in Missouri or Illinois?

Compare the full monthly payment and the commute. Metro East homes often cost less to buy, but Illinois property taxes are higher. Missouri charges personal property tax on vehicles and Illinois doesn't. I'm licensed in both Missouri and Illinois, so you can look on both sides of the river before deciding. That's how Angelina found her forever home.

What is an occupancy permit?

Most towns on both sides of the river inspect a home when it sells and issue a permit before the new owner moves in. Rules, fees and timing vary by town, and some inspections take weeks to schedule, so I check the requirements as soon as we're under contract.

Do I need a home inspection if the house looks fine?

Yes. A home inspection costs a few hundred dollars and can uncover roof, furnace, plumbing or foundation problems you can't see on a tour. It's also how we negotiate repairs or credits. Even in a bidding war I don't push buyers to waive it — Shuai and Linwei's story shows why.

Can I buy a home that needs work?

Yes, but go in with a plan. An inspection and contractor quotes tell you what you're taking on. Some loans also have standards for the home's condition, so we check with your lender first.

Do I need a real estate attorney?

Not usually. On both sides of the river in the St. Louis area, a title company handles the closing and buyers don't typically hire an attorney. I review every contract and deadline with you, and you're always free to hire one if you want.

What happens at closing?

We do a final walk-through, then you sign the loan and title documents, pay your closing funds and get the keys. Plan on about an hour.

How selling your home works

From our first conversation to handing over the keys. I handle the paperwork, scheduling and negotiation so you can focus on your move.

  1. Talk through your goals

    Your timeline, where you're moving, what you need to net from the sale, and anything about the house that worries you.

  2. Price it with real data

    I walk through the home and prepare a comparative market analysis of recent sales, active competition and homes that didn't sell, so you see why a price will work.

  3. Sign the listing agreement and start the paperwork

    We complete your seller's disclosures and, in most towns, apply for the occupancy inspection right away. Some inspections take weeks to schedule.

  4. Prepare the home

    We decide together which repairs and cleanup are worth the money and which aren't. Then come professional photos and the listing write-up.

  5. Go live and show the home

    Your home goes on the MLS, which feeds Zillow, Realtor.com, Homes.com and every agent's search. I schedule showings and send you feedback.

  6. Review offers and negotiate

    I compare offers on more than price: financing strength, contingencies, closing date and what the buyer is asking you to pay. Then I negotiate for the best overall deal.

  7. Inspection, appraisal and closing

    I negotiate the buyer's repair requests, track the appraisal and the buyer's loan, make sure the occupancy permit is done, and get you to the closing table.

Pricing

The price decides how fast you sell and what you net

  • Price to the market, not the Zestimate. Online estimates can't see your updates, your street or the condition inside. Recent sales of similar homes nearby can.
  • The first two weeks matter most. That's when buyers and their agents pay the most attention. A home priced right from day one gets more showings and stronger offers.
  • Overpricing usually costs money. Homes that sit get price cuts, and buyers start asking what's wrong with them.
  • Know your net. I'll show you an estimate of what you walk away with after commissions, closing costs, taxes and any payoff.
Preparing your home

Spend where buyers notice

Most sellers don't need a renovation. They need a clean, bright, well-maintained house in its best light.

  • Fix the small, obvious things. Dripping faucets, burned-out bulbs, sticky doors and peeling paint make buyers wonder what else was skipped.
  • Declutter and deep clean. It costs little and photographs well.
  • Neutral paint where it's needed. Usually the best return of any pre-listing project.
  • Curb appeal. Trim, mulch, a clean front door and working porch lights.
  • Ask before big projects. New kitchens and bathrooms rarely pay back fully before a sale. I'll tell you what buyers in your price range expect.
Mistakes sellers make

What costs sellers time and money

Starting the occupancy permit too late

Most towns on both sides of the river require one before the buyer moves in. St. Louis County inspections can take four to six weeks to schedule, so I apply when we list, not after we have a contract.

Hiding known problems

Disclose what you know. In Illinois the law requires a written disclosure report before the contract is signed. In Missouri, sellers can be liable for concealing known defects. Honest disclosure protects your sale.

Picking the highest price, not the best offer

A slightly lower offer with strong financing, fewer contingencies and no request for closing-cost help can put more money in your pocket and actually close.

Overreacting to the inspection

Every inspection report finds something. Most repair requests are negotiable, and a small credit often keeps a deal together.

Being home for showings

Buyers relax and stay longer when the owner isn't there. Step out, and take pets with you if you can.

Selling before planning your next move

Know where you're going and how the timing works before you list. I can line up the sale and your next purchase together.

What it costs

Costs of selling

Before you list, I'll give you a written net sheet estimating what you'll walk away with. Your title company prepares the final figures.

  • Real estate commissions. Negotiable, and set in writing in the listing agreement. We'll discuss whether to offer compensation to the buyer's agent.
  • Mortgage payoff. Your remaining loan balance, plus interest up to the closing date.
  • Title and closing fees. Charged by the title company that handles the closing.
  • Property taxes. Prorated so you pay for the time you owned the home. Illinois taxes are billed a year behind, so Illinois sellers usually give the buyer a credit at closing for taxes not yet billed.
  • Illinois transfer tax. $0.50 per $500 of the price to the state, plus county tax. Usually paid by the seller. Missouri has none.
  • Repairs, credits and occupancy fixes. Anything agreed after the inspection or required by the town's occupancy inspection.
  • Buyer concessions, if any. Some buyers ask the seller to pay part of their closing costs. It's negotiable.

Seller FAQ

Questions sellers ask me most

If yours isn't here, call or text 314-494-6038.

What is my home worth?

It depends on recent sales of similar homes nearby, your home's condition and updates, and what's competing with it right now. I'll prepare a free comparative market analysis and walk you through it. Request your free home value analysis.

How long will it take to sell?

A well-priced home in good condition often goes under contract within a few weeks, and closing usually takes another 30 to 45 days. Your price, location and the season all affect timing.

What does it cost to sell my home?

Commissions, title and closing fees, your mortgage payoff, prorated property taxes, any agreed repairs or credits, and in Illinois the transfer tax. I'll give you a written net sheet before you list.

Are real estate commissions negotiable?

Yes. Commissions are set in writing in the listing agreement, and we'll also discuss whether to offer compensation to a buyer's agent.

Should I make repairs before I list?

Fix the small, obvious things and clean thoroughly. Big remodels rarely pay for themselves before a sale. I'll tell you what's worth doing for your price range.

Can I sell my house as-is?

Yes. You'll still need to disclose known defects, and most towns still require an occupancy inspection, though some allow the buyer to take on the required repairs. We'll price it to reflect its condition.

Do I need an occupancy permit to sell?

In most towns on both sides of the river, yes. The rules, fees and timing vary by town. I'll check your town's requirements when we list and start the application early.

What do I have to disclose?

In Illinois, sellers must give a written Residential Real Property Disclosure Report before the contract is signed. In Missouri there's no required state form, but you shouldn't conceal known defects. Homes built before 1978 also need a lead-based paint disclosure.

Can I buy my next home before I sell this one?

Often, yes. It depends on your finances. A lender can tell you whether you can carry both, and I can time the sale and purchase together. The Buyer Guide walks through the buying side.

Do I need an attorney to sell?

Not usually. In the St. Louis area a title company handles the closing on both sides of the river, and sellers don't typically hire an attorney.

Twenty questions to ask

Who you are in business with matters! How they treat you is critical to your success. Here are 20 important questions to ask about the company you are currently with, or considering joining:

  1. Does the company allow you to build your own agent brand? Can you put your own logo, name, phone number and website on yard signs and in your ads? Can it be larger than or as prominent as the company's?
  2. Does the company allow you to directly receive the sign calls or inquiries on your own listings without going through the up-desk or office receptionist?
  3. When a person is seeking you on the company's local or national website, can they quickly (within two or three clicks) get directly to you, your own website, and your own business number?
  4. Can you do agent-to-agent referrals without going through the company's relocation division and paying them a portion of your referral fee?
  5. If you decide to leave the company, do they let you take your listings with you? Do they still pay you the same commissions you were owed on your pending transactions — without any penalties or reduction in splits?
  6. Does the company seek your input and advice on important financial and policy decisions? Do you have a say in what goes on?
  7. If you are a top-producing agent, do you get to serve on a council that discusses and votes on company changes in policy and fees?
  8. Does the company open its books and allow you to see and review monthly financial records such as Profit and Loss statements?
  9. Does the company offer you an opportunity to share in its profits? Is the profit-sharing amount greater than 30% of the company's net income?
  10. Do you have the opportunity to earn residual income from the company that will continue even after you leave the company or retire?
  11. Does the company cap the money you pay it on an annual basis (Company Dollar), so that you do not have to keep paying them on all of your production?
  12. If you are affiliated with a national franchise, do they cap your royalties (or service fees) annually, so that you don't have to keep paying them a portion of all your commissions?
  13. Does the company allow you to earn 100% commissions without having to pay monthly fees even when you don't have closings?
  14. Does the company provide a way for you to actually earn more than 100% — to be paid more by the company than it costs you to be with them?
  15. Does the company provide all levels of sales and business training, from the fundamentals of real estate sales all the way up to hiring staff, building a team, managing your expenses and creating a business plan?
  16. Does the company provide wealth-building information and training? Do they give you good advice and consulting on how to gain financial independence?
  17. Do the leaders of the company take the time and make the effort to share with you research, strategies and systems that you can use to improve your business and career?
  18. Have the leaders of the company written best-selling books that are used and respected in the real estate industry? Do they speak with knowledge and authority on the issues that matter to the agents?
  19. Does the company have a set of written beliefs and values that it shares and sincerely seeks to live by? Does the company have a spirit and culture of teamwork and positive energy?
  20. Is the company clearly agent-centered? Does it understand that the consumer selects the agent, not the company? Does that awareness show up in the way the company does business?

Net sheet questions

Why is the Illinois tax credit so large?

Illinois property taxes are paid one year in arrears — the bill you pay this year covers last year. At closing, the seller credits the buyer for all the taxes that have accrued but aren't billed yet, which is usually last year's bill plus this year's share up to the closing date. Missouri prorates only the current year.

Does Missouri or Illinois charge a transfer tax?

Missouri has no real estate transfer tax. Illinois charges a state and county transfer tax of about $1.50 per $1,000 of sale price, paid by the seller in most transactions — about $375 on a $250,000 home.

Are the commissions in this calculator fixed?

No. Commissions are negotiable and not set by law. The defaults here are placeholders — change them to whatever you and your agent agree to, and we'll discuss exactly how this works when we talk.

What about my escrow account?

Any balance in your escrow account is refunded separately by your loan servicer after closing, usually within 30 days. It isn't part of the closing numbers, so it's not in this estimate — think of it as a bonus check that arrives later.

Seller Guide · Preparation

Preparing your home for sale

The goal isn't a renovation — it's removing every reason a buyer would lower their offer. Here's what's worth doing in the St. Louis area, and what isn't.

Refresh, don't renovate

Most sellers either do too little or spend too much on the wrong things. A deep clean, neutral paint and small repairs usually return more than any remodel — buyers pay for a home that feels cared for, not for your taste in countertops. Before you spend a dollar, walk through with me: I'll tell you honestly which projects will show up in your price and which won't.

The prep checklist, in order

  1. Start the occupancy permit early

    Most towns on both sides of the river inspect the home before a new owner moves in. Knocking out those items first means fixing what the town will force you to fix anyway — on your schedule, not during the contract.

  2. Fix the inspection red flags

    Anything a buyer's inspector will flag in the first ten minutes: active leaks, bad gutters, dead outlets, a furnace that hasn't been serviced. These kill deals; countertops don't.

  3. Deep clean and kill the odors

    Buyers decide in the first minute, and smell is half of it. Carpets, pet areas, smoke — handle them before the first showing, because there's no second first impression.

  4. Paint in neutrals

    The cheapest transformation per dollar. Fresh, light, neutral walls make rooms feel bigger, brighter and newer.

  5. Refresh the curb appeal

    Most buyers see your house first as a photo on a phone. Mow, mulch, trim, and make the front door area inviting — it sets the expectation for everything inside.

  6. Small updates, not remodels

    New cabinet hardware, modern light fixtures, updated faucets. Skip the kitchen remodel — you rarely get that money back, and buyers would rather pick their own.

  7. Declutter and depersonalize

    Buyers need to picture their life in the house, not yours. Clear counters, thin out closets, pack the photo wall early — you're moving anyway.

What buyers actually notice

After hundreds of showings with first-time buyers, the pattern is consistent: the listing photos get them in the door, kitchens and baths get the longest look, neutral and clean beats updated and cluttered, odors end showings early, and a clean inspection keeps the deal together. Prepare for those five things and you've done the work that matters.

Sell as-is, or fix first?

Sometimes the right answer is to fix nothing and price accordingly — an estate sale, a house with major systems at end of life, or a seller who needs speed over dollars. That's a math question, not a pride question: we compare what the repairs cost against what they return in your price, and you decide with real numbers. This is part of the free written analysis I prepare for every seller.

How long does prep take?

A well-kept home: a weekend or two. A home with deferred maintenance: plan 30 to 90 days, mostly waiting on contractors — another reason to walk through with me early, even months before you plan to list.

Prep questions sellers ask

Should I remodel my kitchen before selling?

Almost never. Full remodels rarely return their cost at sale, and buyers often prefer choosing their own finishes. Clean, declutter, update hardware and lighting, and let the price reflect the kitchen's age instead.

What should I fix first before listing?

Start with what your town's occupancy inspection will require, then anything a buyer's inspector will flag: leaks, electrical issues, HVAC service, roof and gutter problems. Cosmetics come after function.

Is staging worth it?

Full professional staging makes sense for some vacant homes. For most occupied homes, decluttering, depersonalizing, good light and furniture arranged to show the space get you most of the benefit for free. I'll tell you which camp your home is in when I see it.

How much does it cost to get a home ready to sell?

For a well-kept home, often just elbow grease and a few hundred dollars of paint and hardware. With deferred maintenance it varies too much to guess — which is exactly why the walk-through comes before the spending.

Can you recommend contractors and handymen?

Yes. Through years of transactions and my referral network I keep a current list of painters, handymen, inspectors and contractors my clients have used — ask me.
